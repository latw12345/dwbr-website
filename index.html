03 Context

A gap nearly a century old.

Domestic workers were written out of federal labor laws in the 1930s. The gap is only now being gradually filled.

6
Federal laws excluded them
The NLRA, FLSA, OSHA, Title VII, the ADA, and the Family and Medical Leave Act, domestic workers are excluded from or left uncovered by each one.
1 in 5
Employed women in 19401
At the time of the FLSA's passage, roughly one in five employed women in the United States worked as a domestic worker, one of the largest female occupations in the country.

The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 established minimum wage, overtime, and the forty-hour week for American workers. It also, by deliberate political compromise with Southern Democrats in Congress, excluded two groups, agricultural workers and domestic workers. At the time, domestic workers were overwhelmingly women, and in the South, the majority were Black women. The exclusion was not incidental. It was the price of passage.

The same pattern repeated three years earlier. The Social Security Act of 1935 and the National Labor Relations Act of 1935 had each excluded domestic and agricultural workers from their core protections. Across the foundational pieces of New Deal legislation, the same two workforces were left out. At least sixty percent of the nation's Black population worked in one of these two excluded categories.

Congress partially addressed the FLSA exclusion in 1974, extending minimum wage coverage to some domestic workers, but creating a new "companionship exemption" that left home care workers, companions for the elderly, and live-in workers without overtime protections for another four decades. The Obama administration's Department of Labor narrowed that exemption in 2015, extending overtime to nearly two million home care workers. The federal gap, however, has never been fully closed. No domestic workers law has passed Congress. What you are looking at, state by state, is what organizing has built in the absence of one.

03 The ordinance

Six protections, one precedent.

What Seattle's 2018 ordinance put in writing, and what it established for the first time.

Minimum wage
Seattle minimum wage applies in full, regardless of whether a worker is classified as an employee or an independent contractor. No other domestic worker law in the country had erased that distinction before Seattle.
Rest & meal breaks
30-minute unpaid meal break after 5 consecutive hours, 10-minute paid rest break for every 4 hours worked. If breaks are not feasible, employers must pay additional compensation. Live-in workers get 24 unpaid hours off after working more than 6 consecutive days.
Written agreements
Employers must provide a written work agreement stating pay, schedule, duties, and conditions, in the language the worker is most comfortable reading or speaking. The city makes model agreements available in over 30 languages.
Document protections
Employers are prohibited from retaining workers' passports, Social Security cards, birth certificates, or other personal documents, a practice used to control live-in workers. The prohibition applies regardless of immigration status.
Anti-retaliation
Employers may not threaten, report, or penalize workers for asserting their rights. Explicitly named as retaliation, threatening to contact immigration authorities. Violations are enforceable by the city, with penalties up to $5,000 for proven retaliation.
Standards Board
A 13-member board, six workers or worker representatives, six employers or hiring entity representatives, one community member, empowered to recommend standards on wages, benefits, and working conditions to the City Council. Washington's 2026 law created the same structure at state scale.

02 The road here

Six years, one passage.

The 2026 bill was not the first attempt, and it was not the compromise version. It added wage guarantees, overtime, and real enforcement power that the 2020 bill never touched.

2020 · HB 2511
Passed both chambers. Died in concurrence.
House 59–39 · Senate 48–1
2026 · HB 2355
Passed both chambers. Signed into law.
House 57–39 · Senate 28–20
Core identity
A civil-rights and anti-retaliation bill. Wage and hour protections were explicitly deferred to a future study committee.
A wage-and-hour bill that directly amends Washington's Minimum Wage Act, with civil-rights protections built in.
Minimum wage
Not addressed. Domestic workers remained exempt from the state Minimum Wage Act under existing law.
Guaranteed for all domestic workers, including live-in workers previously excluded from coverage. Workers are entitled to the state minimum wage ($17.13/hr in 2026).
Overtime
Not addressed. Domestic workers had no legal entitlement to overtime pay.
Required. 1.5 times the regular rate for all hours worked over 40 in a week.
Written agreement
Voluntary. The Attorney General developed a model template in 8 or more languages, but employers were not required to provide or use one.
Mandatory before work begins. Must be in a language the worker can read or understand. L&I provides a model template.
Termination & severance
Not addressed. Workers could be dismissed without notice or compensation.
Employers must give at least 2 weeks of written notice, or 4 weeks for live-in workers. If notice is not given, severance pay is required instead.
Enforcement
Private lawsuits only. No state agency had authority to investigate complaints or penalize employers.
L&I can investigate complaints, issue citations, and levy civil penalties of $1,000 minimum, rising to $20,000–$40,000 for repeat violations. Workers retain the private right to sue as well.
Definition
A descriptive list of job types (nannies, house cleaners, home care workers, cooks, gardeners) with no minimum hours threshold. Without a clear cutoff, opponents could dispute who qualified, an ambiguity that stalled every subsequent version of the bill through 2025.
The same job-type list, now anchored by a clear threshold: anyone paid for 4 or more hours of work in a private home per month qualifies. This resolved the definitional dispute that caused the 2025 Senate-passed version to die in the House Rules Committee.

04 The workers

Who this reaches.

An estimated 100,000 people in Washington do this work. Starting July 1, 2027, it comes with the same baseline protections as nearly every other job in the state.

They are nannies, housekeepers, gardeners, cooks, and home or personal care aides, caring for children, families, and homes across the state. For most of them, that work has never carried the protections that apply to nearly every other job in Washington. Starting July 1, 2027, that changes. Domestic workers are entitled to the state minimum wage, overtime for hours worked over 40 in a week, and a written agreement before a job even begins. And if an employer does not honor it, there is now a state agency with real authority to hear that complaint.
